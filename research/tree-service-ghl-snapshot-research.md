## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Tree service companies are one of the highest-value, lowest-tech verticals in home services. The average tree removal job runs $750–$3,000+. Emergency storm removals can be $2,500–$10,000+ per job. Yet most tree service operators answer their phone from a bucket truck, lose leads in voicemail, have zero follow-up system, and let storm-surge demand evaporate within 72 hours.

This snapshot targets a $67B+ (US) tree care industry that is **wide open for automation.** The 1app GHL snapshot for tree service should be the most complete, most industry-specific system available — purpose-built around the way tree crews actually work, the way homeowners actually make decisions, and the way storms actually generate business.

## PART 1: BUSINESS PAIN POINTS — HOW TREE COMPANIES ACTUALLY SUFFER

### 1.1 Missed Calls = Lost Revenue

Tree service is one of the highest "impulse" home service verticals. When a homeowner has a dead limb hanging over their roof after a storm, they call three companies. Whoever answers wins. Most tree companies:
- Run skeleton office staff (or no office staff at all)
- Have owners and crew in the field, chippers running, no phone signal in canopy
- Miss 30–50% of inbound calls
- Lose 70%+ of those leads permanently (no callback system)

**The automation gap:** A missed call text-back within 60 seconds wins back 40–60% of those lost leads. Most tree companies have literally nothing.

### 1.2 Seasonal Demand Swings Are Brutal

Tree service demand spikes unpredictably:
- **Storm events** — 10x call volume in 24–72 hours after major wind, ice, or snow events
- **Spring flush** — canopy assessment, crown lifting, structural pruning after dormancy
- **Late fall** — leaf drop, hazard tree identification before snow load season
- **Summer drought/heat stress** — dead limb removal, crown reduction to reduce water demand

In the dead of winter or peak summer heat, calls drop. The companies that survive and scale are the ones that have systems to **harvest storm demand fast** and **manufacture demand in slow periods** through annual service reminders.

### 1.3 No Estimate Follow-Up

The typical tree service estimate workflow:
1. Arborist or crew leader visits site
2. Scribbles numbers on a notepad or texts a number
3. Says "let me know" and drives away
4. Never follows up

Industry average: 30–40% of tree service estimates are accepted. With a structured follow-up workflow (3-touch over 7 days), that number jumps to 55–65%. Most companies leave this on the table completely.

### 1.4 Emergency Work Is Chaos

Post-storm emergency dispatch is a nightmare without automation:
- Phones ringing off the hook from multiple numbers
- No triage system — a 90-foot oak on a power line gets the same queue as a neighbor wanting a shrub trimmed
- No way to collect job location, access details, or urgency level before dispatch
- Insurance documentation requirements create delays when crew arrives

### 1.5 Zero Repeat Business Mechanism

A residential customer who gets a tree removed has a **high probability of needing tree service again within 2–3 years** (stump grinding follow-up, other trees need assessment, storm damage). But:
- Tree companies have no CRM
- Customer data lives in paper invoices or a spreadsheet
- Nobody calls existing clients in spring or before storm season

**The annual reminder campaign is pure money.** Clients who had a job 12–18 months ago need a touchpoint. Almost no tree companies do this.

### 1.6 Permit & Protected Tree Compliance Creates Friction

Urban and suburban markets increasingly have tree bylaws:
- Protected species (heritage trees, large-caliper trees above threshold DBH)
- Tree removal permits required in many municipalities
- ISA Certified Arborist reports required for permit applications
- HOA approval required in many developments

When companies aren't upfront about permit requirements, it creates project delays, client frustration, and scope-of-work disputes. An intake form that captures tree species, approximate DBH (diameter at breast height), and location relative to property lines can pre-qualify permit needs.

### 1.7 Crew Scheduling & Equipment Logistics

Tree service is operationally complex:
- Large jobs (100+ foot canopy removal, lot clearing) require chipper truck, bucket truck, skid steer, and 4–6 crew
- Small jobs (stump grinding, single limb removal) require one grinder operator
- Routing matters — aerial lift equipment can't go 45 minutes between jobs efficiently
- Weather cancellations are common — need to reschedule efficiently

Most companies use text messages and verbal communication for scheduling. No system to batch jobs by location, equipment need, or crew size.

### 1.8 Insurance Claims — A Missed Revenue Stream

Storm-damaged trees with structural damage to property trigger homeowners insurance claims. A tree service that knows how to:
- Document tree damage with timestamped photos
- Write a scope-of-work that aligns with insurance adjuster language
- Work directly with insurance adjusters

...can command premium rates and get referrals from insurance adjusters. Most tree companies walk away from this.

### 1.9 Commercial & Municipal Work Is Underserved

Property managers (HOAs, commercial parks, apartment complexes, shopping centers) and municipalities (parks departments, roadway maintenance, utility right-of-way clearing) are consistent, volume clients. But winning this work requires:
- Professional proposals with formal scope of work
- Liability insurance certificates
- ISA Certified Arborist on staff or on call
- Ability to handle multi-season contracts

Most small tree companies never pursue this. The snapshot should include a commercial intake workflow.

## PART 4: WORKFLOWS

### 4.1 Missed Call Text-Back (MCTB) — Core Workflow

**Trigger:** Missed inbound call to business number  
**Delay:** 30–60 seconds  

**SMS #1 (immediate):**
> "Hey, this is [Company Name]! Sorry we missed you — we're probably up in a tree right now 🌳 We'll call you back shortly. In the meantime, reply with what you need and we'll get you sorted!"

**If no reply in 5 minutes — SMS #2:**
> "Still here! If you need a free estimate for tree removal, trimming, or emergency work, just reply or book online: [booking link]"

**If they reply with keyword "emergency" or "storm" → trigger Emergency Dispatch Workflow**

**If call answered within workflow window → mark MCTB resolved, no further SMS**

**Trigger:** Inbound form, MCTB emergency keyword, or manual tag "EMERGENCY"

## APPENDIX A: INDUSTRY TERMINOLOGY REFERENCE

Use these terms in templates and documentation to establish industry credibility:

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **DBH** | Diameter at Breast Height — standard measurement of tree trunk diameter at 4.5 feet above grade |
| **Crown Reduction** | Reducing overall crown size by pruning back branch terminals to lateral branches — reduces wind load and tree height |
| **Crown Lifting** | Removing lower branches to raise the crown base — improves clearance over roads, structures, lawn |
| **Crown Thinning** | Selective removal of branches within the crown to increase light penetration and air movement |
| **Deadwood Removal** | Removing dead, dying, or diseased branches from the crown — reduces weight and hazard |
| **Widow Maker** | A large dead limb suspended in the canopy — can fall without warning, serious hazard |
| **Root Flare** | The expanding base of the trunk where it meets the root system — should be visible above grade |
| **Girdling Root** | A root that circles the trunk and gradually constricts the vascular system — eventually fatal |
| **Included Bark** | Bark embedded in a branch attachment, indicating a weak union — failure risk under load |
| **Co-dominant Stems** | Two stems of equal diameter growing from a common point — high failure risk at union |
| **Root Plate** | The lateral root system that anchors the tree — failure of root plate = tree topple |
| **Hazard Tree** | A tree that has been assessed as posing risk of failure and potential injury/property damage |
| **Canopy** | The upper layer of a tree's branches and foliage |
| **Drip Line** | The outermost edge of the canopy — roughly corresponds to root extent |
| **Topping** | The improper removal of large portions of the crown — an ISA-condemned practice |
| **ISA Arborist** | International Society of Arboriculture Certified Arborist — industry credential |
| **ANSI A300** | Industry standard for tree care practices (pruning, cabling, tree assessment) |
| **Lot Clearing** | Complete removal of all trees, brush, and stumps from a parcel for development |
| **Chipping** | Processing brush and small-diameter wood through a chipper for mulch |
| **Wood Chips** | Mulch product from chipped branches — often left on site or used in gardens |
| **Stump Grinding** | Mechanical removal of tree stumps using a grinding wheel — typically to 6–12 inches below grade |
| **Root Flare Excavation** | Air spade or manual excavation to expose buried root flares — often reveals girdling roots |
| **Cabling & Bracing** | Installing support systems (steel cables, rods) to reinforce structurally compromised trees |
| **Tree Inventory** | A systematic assessment and documentation of all trees on a property |
| **Hazard Assessment** | Formal evaluation of tree risk using ISA or TRAQ methodology |
| **TRAQ** | Tree Risk Assessment Qualification — ISA credential for formal risk assessment |
| **Emerald Ash Borer** | Invasive insect destroying ash trees across North America |
| **Oak Wilt** | Fungal disease killing oak trees — spreads through root connections |
| **Dutch Elm Disease** | Fungal disease responsible for near-elimination of American Elm |
| **Canker** | A necrotic area on the bark/wood caused by fungal or bacterial infection |
| **Storm Damage** | Physical damage to trees from wind, ice, snow, lightning, or flooding |
| **Aerial Lift / Bucket Truck** | Hydraulic lift used to access high canopy work — required for large trees |
| **Skid Steer** | Compact tracked equipment for moving heavy wood and brush on site |
| **Right-of-Way (ROW) Clearing** | Vegetation removal along roads, power lines, and utility corridors |
